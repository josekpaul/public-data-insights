What this number actually is

GDP per capita is total economic output produced within a place, divided by the number of people who live there — not household income, not take-home pay, not what a typical resident actually earns. It counts corporate profits, government spending, capital investment, and business activity of every kind, whether or not any of it ever reaches a household budget. Median household income is a different, and for most places meaningfully lower, number: it's counted per household rather than per person, it reflects what actually lands in residents' pockets rather than total output, and it isn't pulled down by a long tail of very poor households the way an average can be pulled up by a short tail of very rich ones. Purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusts for the fact that a dollar goes further in a place with lower prices, so a given PPP figure represents comparable buying power across countries even though local prices and currencies differ.

The gap between output and income is exactly why the entities in the section below rank where they do: DC, Ireland, Singapore, Luxembourg, Qatar, Macao and Guyana all have GDP figures inflated by production or profit that doesn't belong to their resident population — commuters, multinational accounting, or resource rents that flow mostly to foreign investors and governments. Reading this ranking as a measure of who's "richest" works reasonably well in the middle of the table, where output and resident income track each other fairly closely; it works much less well at the extremes.

Why states belong on the same scale as countries

The international dollar is defined so that one buys, in any country, what one US dollar buys in the United States. That means US GDP per capita in PPP terms already equals US GDP per capita in nominal dollars — and a state's current-dollar GDP per capita is therefore already on the same international-dollar scale as every country in this table, with no conversion needed. The one caveat: that equivalence holds at the national average US price level, and prices actually run from about 87 in Arkansas and Mississippi to about 111 in California and Hawaii (BEA Regional Price Parities, 2024). The RPP-adjust toggle below restates each state at the US average, which is the more careful comparison; the unadjusted figure is what's usually quoted and is shown by default.

Taiwan is the one exception to the sourcing: it has no World Bank entry at all, so its figure comes from the IMF instead and isn't perfectly methodologically aligned with the rest of the table.

Reading the anomalies

11 entities flagged in the table below

GDP per capita measures output produced within a jurisdiction's borders, divided by its resident population — not resident income. Wherever a large share of that output is produced by non-residents, booked there for tax reasons, or extracted as a resource rent that mostly leaves the country, the figure runs well ahead of what people who actually live there take home. These are the clearest cases in the table; look for the flagged circle next to an entity's name for the same note inline.

District of ColumbiaCommuting artifact
Ranks #1 at $277,689 — but roughly 500,000 people commute in daily from Maryland and Virginia. Their output lands in DC's numerator; they're counted in another jurisdiction's population. DC's median household income is far below what this figure implies.
IrelandProfit-booking
Ranks #4 at $155,089. Multinational IP transfers (a 26% single-year GDP jump in 2015) and aircraft-leasing profits are booked here for tax reasons. Ireland's own GNI* measure, designed to strip this out, runs about 45% lower than GDP — which would put it closer to 30th, not 4th.
GuyanaOil windfall, uneven
Ranks #18 at $97,899, and unlike Ireland this is real physical output — ExxonMobil's Stabroek discovery took Guyana to roughly 650,000 barrels/day across 800,000 people. But the production-sharing agreement lets the Exxon-Hess-CNOOC consortium recover costs and take profit oil first, so much of that income leaves the country; poverty remains widespread, especially in hinterland and Indigenous communities.
Singapore, LuxembourgHub economies
Rank #2 ($163,354) and #3 ($156,542). Both are financial/trade hubs whose output reflects cross-border activity and, for Luxembourg, a workforce that substantially commutes in from France, Germany and Belgium — the same population/output mismatch as DC, at a national scale.
Qatar, BruneiResource rents
Rank #6 ($128,883) and #23 ($92,430). LNG and oil revenue divided by small populations that are substantially non-citizen migrant labor rather than a broad domestic economy.
Macao SAR, ChinaCasino/tourism revenue
Ranks #5 at $137,031. Gaming and tourism revenue from non-resident visitors dominates GDP relative to the small resident population — the same shape as Qatar's rents, sourced from casinos instead of gas fields.
TaiwanDifferent source
Ranks #21 at $93,700. Absent from World Bank WDI entirely, so this figure is an IMF estimate instead — the only entity in the table not on the same underlying methodology as the rest.
MississippiWidest US spread
The poorest US state at $55,877, ranking 89th globally — still just ahead of Japan. The gap from Mississippi to DC ($277,689) is wider than the gap from Mississippi to most of the countries below it.

Methodology. Country figures are World Bank World Development Indicators, indicator NY.GDP.PCAP.PP.CD, reference year 2025, database vintage last updated 2026-07-13 — 185 entities have a 2025 value: 181 sovereign countries and 4 territories, with PPPs from the ICP 2021 round. Regional and income-group aggregates were excluded. Taiwan, absent from World Bank WDI, is added from the IMF World Economic Outlook (April 2026), PPPPC, 2025 — the only entity not on the WDI series. US state figures are BEA current-dollar GDP by state (SAGDP2N, 2025, vintage released 2026-04-09) divided by Census Bureau resident population (Vintage 2025 / NST-EST2025, July 1 2025); both were read from FRED's republication of the same series, since BEA and Census now publish these tables only through JavaScript interactive apps or key-gated APIs. The RPP-adjusted column restates state figures using BEA Regional Price Parities (all items, US=100, 2024, the latest year published) — a reasonable correction, not a like-for-like PPP conversion, since RPPs are a US-internal index on a different basket than the ICP's.

This measures output produced within a jurisdiction, per resident — not household income. The "reading the anomalies" section above lists the clearest cases where that gap is large; PPP estimates in general carry real uncertainty, and gaps of a few percent between adjacent ranks aren't meaningful. Thirteen countries have no published 2025 World Bank figure and are simply absent: Afghanistan, Cuba, Eritrea, Lebanon, Liechtenstein, Monaco, North Korea, San Marino, South Sudan, Syria, the United Arab Emirates, Venezuela, and Yemen. Most small territories are missing too — Bermuda, the Cayman Islands, the Channel Islands, the Faroe Islands, Greenland, Guam, the Isle of Man, and the US Virgin Islands among them. The "United States" row is national data and overlaps the 51 state rows below it — it's kept as a benchmark, not counted as an additional independent entity. The 51 state GDP figures sum to slightly less than national GDP because BEA doesn't allocate federal activity abroad, or the statistical discrepancy, to individual states.
